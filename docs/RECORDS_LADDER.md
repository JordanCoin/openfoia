# The records ladder

Which rung of the US public-records system holds your record, and how to ask
for it. Written for a reporter or researcher who just learned that every state
has its own law. This is a working guide, not legal advice.

Every claim below is tagged. **Fetched** means someone on this project read the
source page on 2026-10-13 or 2026-10-14. **Snippet** means only a search-result
summary was seen. **Unverified** means it comes from memory and no page was
checked. Treat unverified items as leads, not facts, and check the statute
before you rely on one.

## The ladder in one picture

```
 5  Non-FOIA paths            NARA, Mandatory Declassification Review,
    (no request letter)       presidential libraries, congressional
                              publications, CREST and its mirrors
                                        ^
 4  Federal FOIA              5 U.S.C. 552: executive agencies only
    (20 working days)         FBI, CIA, DOJ, Education, NIH ...
                                        ^
 3  State agencies and        state education department, state archive
    state archives            (same state law as rung 2, or a research request)
                                        ^
 2  The state law             50 states + DC, each with its own deadline,
    (governs rungs 1 and 3)   fees, residency rule, appeal path
                                        ^
 1  The public body that      school district, city, county, police,
    made the record           public university: ask ITS records custodian
```

The rung you are on decides who you write to, what statute you cite, how
long they have, what they may charge, and where you appeal. Most records about
local programs never climb above rung 2. Most records about federal agencies
never come down below rung 4. Do not blend them in one request or one case.

## Rung 1: the public body that made the record

**Who holds what.** A school district, a city, a county, a police department,
a public university, a special district. Each is a separate public body with
its own records custodian. A district is not the state education department
and not the city it sits in. If the body was merged or renamed, the successor
body holds the records.

**How to ask.** Write to the body's records custodian or FOIA coordinator
(district central office, county clerk, city clerk, police records unit,
university records office). Cite the state statute (rung 2), describe the
record types, give a date range, ask for electronic copies, ask for an
itemized fee estimate before work starts, and ask that any withheld record be
identified with the exemption cited.

**The clock and the fees** are set by the state law, rung 2. There is no
federal path to a local body. Federal FOIA does not apply.

**Whether the record still exists** is decided by the state's records
retention schedule, not by goodwill. Board minutes are permanent almost
everywhere. Program files, vendor contracts and staff memos often carry 5 to
10 year periods and may have been lawfully destroyed. Michigan's schedule for
public schools is General Retention Schedule #2, revised 2023 (fetched:
https://www.michigan.gov/-/media/Project/Websites/dtmb/Services/Records-Management/RMS_GS2.pdf;
index of local schedules:
https://www.michigan.gov/dtmb/services/recordsmanagement/schedules/GSLocal).
Texas uses Local Schedule SD for school districts (snippet:
https://www.tsl.texas.gov/slrm/localretention/schedule_sd). The move when a
clerk says "we don't have that": ask for the retention schedule series they
applied, the destruction log or certificate with its date, and a written
description of the search they ran.

**The FERPA boundary.** FERPA (20 U.S.C. 1232g) protects individual student
records, so rosters and scores tied to a student are blocked. It does not
cover program design, policies, screening criteria, which test instruments
were bought, contracts, evaluations or board minutes. Aggregate counts are
releasable when no student can be identified; the test is whether a
reasonable person in the school community could identify a student (34 CFR
99.3 and 99.31(b)(1), snippet:
https://studentprivacy.ed.gov/resources/data-de-identification-overview-basic-terms).
Small cells get suppressed; thresholds of 3 to 10 per cell are common
(snippet). Ask for program and aggregate records and the clerk cannot deny
the whole request on student privacy.

**What goes wrong.** No FOIA staff, so the request lands on a clerk. Fee
estimates used as a deterrent: an Iowa Public Information Board case decided
in 2025 involved a $100 per hour staff-time quote before the bodies agreed to
revised fee schedules (snippet:
https://ipib.iowa.gov/25fc0022-steve-st-clair-v-winneshiek-county-and-city-ossian-final-report-0).
"No records" with no documented search. Electronic records never searched.
Email deleted on short schedules. Old paper in a basement nobody indexed.
A University of Florida Brechner Center study reported high fees, rudeness
and privacy claims as the common local barriers (snippet, date unconfirmed:
https://brechner.jou.ufl.edu/?p=4063).

## Rung 2: the state law

All 50 states and DC have a public-records statute: Michigan FOIA, the
California Public Records Act, the Texas Public Information Act, New York
FOIL, Florida Chapter 119, the Washington Public Records Act, and so on. They
differ on every axis that matters. The Reporters Committee for Freedom of the
Press keeps one page per state in a standard outline (fetched:
https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-guide/). What the project confirmed from
those pages:

| Axis | Confirmed examples (fetched from the RCFP state page) |
|------|-------------------------------------------------------|
| Deadline | Michigan: 5 business days, one written extension of up to 10 business days. Illinois: 5 business days; a late response forfeits copy fees. Texas: "promptly", with a written certification and a date if production will take more than 10 business days (Gov't Code 552.221(d)). New York, California, Florida and Washington deadlines were not reached in the fetched text (unverified). |
| Fees | Illinois: first 50 pages free, then up to 15 cents a page, no search or review charges. Washington: 15 cents a page, 10 cents a scan, no search fees. Florida: actual duplication cost or 15 cents, plus a "special service fee" for extensive work. Michigan: labor at the lowest-paid capable employee's wage in 15-minute increments, deposit allowed above $50. New York: 25 cents a photocopy. |
| Who enforces | New York: the Committee on Open Government is advisory only; appeals are Article 78 proceedings in state Supreme Court. Michigan: no commission; denials go to circuit court within 180 days, fee disputes to the head of the body within 10 days or the Court of Claims within 45. Texas: the Attorney General rules on exemptions, within 45 business days. Washington: optional non-binding AG review (RCW 42.56.530), then suit (RCW 42.56.550). Florida: voluntary AG mediation (Fla. Stat. 16.60), then mandamus. Illinois: the Public Access Counselor issues opinions. Connecticut, Pennsylvania and New Jersey have independent bodies (unverified this pass). |
| Residency | Texas ("anyone, citizens and non-citizens alike"), Florida (citizenship requirement removed in 1975) and California ("every person") take requests from anyone. Michigan, Illinois and Washington text showed no limit. Virginia's citizens-only rule was upheld in McBurney v. Young, 569 U.S. 221 (2013); a snippet says Arkansas, Tennessee and Virginia still restrict, and that most other states repealed theirs (unverified: https://www.scotusblog.com/?p=410225). Check before filing from out of state. |
| Who is covered | Usually state agencies, counties, cities, school districts, police and public universities. Private universities usually not. Michigan exempts its own governor and legislature; reform bills passed the Michigan Senate 33-2 in early 2025 and stalled in the House (snippet: https://michiganadvance.com/2026/01/18/were-just-not-going-to-do-foia-michigan-transparency-effort-stalls/). |

**Two enforcement models.** Some states route appeals through an independent
body (a commission, an attorney general, a public access counselor). Others
send you straight to court. The model decides whether an appeal costs a
letter or a lawyer.

**No dataset exists.** The RCFP guide is HTML only. The NFOIC state-laws page
returned 404 and MuckRock's jurisdiction API returned 403 to the project's
fetches, so neither was confirmed as a data source. A tool that wants all 51
laws has to build its own table from the RCFP outline and the statutes.

**Scorecards.** No current, credible ranking was found. The last broad ones
are the 2007-08 BGA/NFOIC study and the 2012 State Integrity Investigation
(snippet: https://nfoic.org/?p=1320). A 2026 "State of Access" report card
exists (https://seeingeye.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/seeing-eye-state-of-access-v4.html)
but its source was not evaluated (unverified).

## Rung 3: state agencies and state archives

**State agencies** answer under the same state law as rung 2 but hold
different files. For a school program that means the state education
department: grant files, program approvals, annual reports from districts.
In Michigan the education FOIA office now sits under MiLEAP as of
December 2023 (snippet: https://www.michigan.gov/mileap/inside-mileap/foia-and-transparency;
older page https://www.michigan.gov/mde/resources/foia).

**State archives** take in permanent state-agency records and some local
records after their retention period. You access them by research request,
not by FOIA, and their catalogs are separate from the agency's FOIA path.
Michigan's schedule names transfers to the Archives of Michigan (fetched,
GS2 above).

**What goes wrong.** The state trail can be thin. Michigan's gifted statute,
the Education for the Gifted and/or Academically Talented Act, Act 299 of
1974, was repealed in 2016 (snippet:
https://renderpdf.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/archive/2019/June/mcl-Act-299-of-1974.pdf).
State funding ran through School Aid Act Section 57 grants to intermediate
districts (snippet: https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/mcl/pdf/mcl-388-1657-amended.pdf),
last funded in 2006-07 at about $285,000 (snippet:
https://www.metroparent.com/?p=10404). Grant files may exist; program design
probably sits with the district.

## Rung 4: federal FOIA

**Who is covered.** Executive-branch agencies and their components. Not
Congress, not the federal courts, not the President's immediate staff, not
state or local bodies, not contractors except for records in an agency's
possession (fetched: https://www.foia.gov/about.html; the DOJ guide at
https://www.justice.gov/oip/doj-guide-freedom-information-act-0). Anyone may
file, including non-citizens.

**How to ask.** File with the component, not the department: the FBI, not
"DOJ". FOIA.gov lists contacts and has a portal form for participating
agencies. The request must "reasonably describe" the records: subject, date
range, office, record type. Your requester category sets fees; news media and
educational requesters pay no search fees and get 100 free pages (fetched:
https://www.foia.gov/faq.html).

**The clocks.** 20 working days to respond, plus 10 for "unusual
circumstances", plus tolling while the agency waits on a clarification or a
fee answer. Expedited processing is decided within 10 calendar days.
Administrative appeal within at least 90 days of a denial, decided in 20
working days. Then non-binding mediation at OGIS (https://www.archives.gov/ogis)
or suit in federal district court, where a blown deadline counts as
exhaustion. The 2016 FOIA Improvement Act added the 90-day floor, the
"foreseeable harm" standard, and the rule that an agency that misses its
deadline loses search fees (https://www.congress.gov/bill/114th-congress/senate-bill/337).

**The exemptions.** b1 classified, b2 internal rules, b3 other statutes, b4
trade secrets, b5 privileged deliberations, b6 personal privacy, b7 law
enforcement (six sub-parts), b8 bank reports, b9 well data. Three exclusions
under 552(c) let an agency answer as if a record does not exist. A Glomar
response refuses to confirm or deny.

**The reality.** From the FY2025 annual FOIA reports summarized by DOJ OIP
(fetched: https://www.justice.gov/oip/blog/summary-fiscal-year-2025-annual-foia-reports-published):

| FY2025 | Figure |
|--------|--------|
| Requests received | 1,707,197 (up 13.7 percent) |
| Requests processed | 1,635,055 |
| Backlog | 339,671 (up 28.2 percent from 264,816) |
| Appeals received | 32,059 (up 59 percent) |
| Full-time FOIA staff | 4,823, down 14.3 percent (OGIS: https://www.archives.gov/ogis/about-ogis/annual-reports/ogis-2026-annual-report-for-fy-2025-appendix-b) |
| DHS share of all requests | about 60 percent |

A New York Times analysis reported by FOIA Advisor found only 47 percent of
requests released anything, the lowest since 2010, and pending requests
waiting more than 240 days on average (fetched:
https://www.foiaadvisor.com/foia-blog/2026/7/29/foia-news-nyt-report-on-foia-requests-in-limbo-or-unfulfilled).
Complex FBI and CIA requests commonly run months to years (unverified; pull
per-agency medians from https://www.foia.gov/data.html).

**What goes wrong.** Vague requests get bounced. "No responsive records"
often means a narrow search of the wrong office or system; challenge the
adequacy of the search on appeal. Referrals and consultations between
agencies add months. "Still interested?" letters close the request if you do
not answer. Nobody tracks the 20-day clock, the tolling events or the 90-day
appeal window for you.

**Two cousins of FOIA.** A Privacy Act request (5 U.S.C. 552a) covers records
about yourself; file it together with FOIA. Mandatory Declassification Review
(E.O. 13526 section 3.5) asks an agency to declassify a specific classified
record; it has no court path but an appeal to the Interagency Security
Classification Appeals Panel, and it can beat FOIA for old CIA records
because it avoids the b3 and Glomar fights (unverified details:
https://www.archives.gov/isoo/policy-documents/eo-13526).

## Rung 5: paths that are not FOIA at all

- **NARA.** Once an agency transfers records to the National Archives, you
  ask NARA, not the agency. The National Archives Catalog API needs a free,
  read-only key; the default allowance is 10,000 queries a month (fetched:
  https://www.archives.gov/research/catalog/help/api). The Swagger page at
  https://catalog.archives.gov/api/v2/api-docs/ rendered only a shell, so the
  endpoint names were not read (unverified). Record groups such as RG 263
  (CIA), RG 65 (FBI) and RG 12 (Office of Education) are from memory
  (unverified).
- **CREST.** The CIA Records Search Tool: records reviewed under the 25-year
  automatic declassification program. About 930,000 documents went online on
  2017-01-17 (snippet: https://intelligencecommunitynews.com/cia-posts-more-than-12-million-pages-of-crest-records-online/).
  The reading room page loads in a browser (fetched:
  https://www.cia.gov/readingroom/collection/crest-25-year-program-archive),
  and the project's own HTTP client is blocked on every reading-room path by
  a bot filter, so search from a script reports "blocked". DocumentCloud
  mirrors much of CREST, and that mirror is where the project's own CIA
  research came from.
- **Presidential libraries.** NARA runs them. Records from 1981 on are
  requested through FOIA under the Presidential Records Act; earlier ones are
  governed by donor deeds (unverified).
- **Congress.** Never subject to FOIA, but its publications are open:
  hearings and reports on https://www.govinfo.gov (API key via
  https://www.govinfo.gov/api-signup, docs https://api.govinfo.gov/docs,
  fetched) and https://www.congress.gov. The Church Committee reports
  (1975-76), the Rockefeller Commission (1975) and the 1977 Senate hearing
  on Project MKULTRA are published sources (unverified citations).
- **Mirrors and finders.** DocumentCloud (search and per-page text; the
  project uses its page-text endpoint daily), MuckRock's request archive,
  the Internet Archive, the Black Vault, HathiTrust for government
  publications. Which have APIs and which are scrape-only was only partly
  confirmed (unverified beyond DocumentCloud).

## Worked example: a 1990s gifted program in Detroit

The question "did a 1990s Detroit public-school gifted program use a
particular screening instrument, and who designed or evaluated it" lives on
rungs 1 to 3. Nothing about it is federal.

| Rung | Custodian | Ask for |
|------|-----------|---------|
| 1 | Detroit Public Schools Community District FOIA coordinator. Online form at https://www.detroitk12.org/Page/7204 (snippet), email foia.request@detroitk12.org, or mail to Office of the General Counsel, ATTN: FOIA Coordinator, 3011 W. Grand Blvd, Suite 1002, Detroit MI 48202 (snippet). | Board minutes, agendas and board packets, 1990 to 1999, mentioning "gifted", "talented", "enrichment" or the program's name, plus the policy adopting it. Program plans, handbooks, identification and screening procedures, and the names of the test instruments adopted. Contracts, purchase orders and invoices with test publishers, consultants and outside evaluators. Program evaluations, annual reports, and correspondence with the program coordinator or any university partner. The retention schedule in force and the destruction log for those series, with a description of the search. Aggregate counts only, no student data. |
| 2 | Michigan FOIA, MCL 15.231 to 15.246. Five business days plus one 10-day extension. Labor billed at the lowest-paid capable employee's wage. No residency limit. Appeal to the head of the body or circuit court (fetched: https://www.rcfp.org/open-government-guide/michigan/). | Cite it in the first 250 words and in the subject line so a non-response counts as a denial. |
| 3 | MiLEAP FOIA (education), MiLEAP-FOIA@michigan.gov (snippet). Archives of Michigan for transferred records. | Section 57 gifted and talented grant files and any district reports under Act 299 of 1974, 1990 to 1999. |
| 1, universities | Wayne State University FOIA, foia@wayne.edu (snippet: https://generalcounsel.wayne.edu/legal/foia). University of Michigan FOIA (snippet: https://foia.vpcomm.umich.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/18/2022/06/FOIA-Procedures-and-Guidelines-6-1-22.pdf). | Agreements, evaluations or research involving the district's gifted program; a named professor's departmental files. |
| Archives | The Reuther Library's Detroit Board of Education / Detroit Public Schools collection, accession 681, covers 1860 to 1980 with bound minutes, reports and budgets (snippet: https://reuther.wayne.edu/files/WSR000681.pdf). It stops at 1980. | Ask the Reuther whether post-1980 additions exist, and ask DPSCD's board office where the 1990s minutes physically sit. The Detroit Public Library's Burton Historical Collection and U-M's Bentley Historical Library are unconfirmed holders. |

Two warnings. The 1999 to 2005 state takeover and the 2009 to 2016 emergency
management may have moved or lost district records (unverified). And the
state trail is thin because the statute is repealed and the funding line
ended in 2006-07.

**Keep the CIA question in its own case.** The separate question "was the
CIA's Personality Assessment System ever given to minors" lives on rungs 4
and 5: CREST via DocumentCloud, a FOIA or MDR filing to the CIA for a record
the index names but the mirror lacks (MORI 144765, a 1952 Detroit trip
report). Its evidence standard, its clocks and its custodians have nothing in
common with a school district's. A case that mixes them produces a report in
which a 1950s CIA memo sits next to a 1990s board minute as if they were the
same trail. The project's own run of the CIA question found nothing in 39
primary documents; that result stands on its own and should not be used to
colour the district question either way.

| Rung | Today on main | In progress |
|------|---------------|-------------|
| 1 | Nothing. The agency table holds federal agencies only; there is no way to record a school district, a city or a university as a public body. | A jurisdictions pull request adds local public bodies tied to a state. |
| 2 | Nothing. One federal deadline rule; templates cite federal FOIA. | A jurisdictions data package (statute, clock, fees, residency, appeal path per state, each entry marked verified or not), per-state templates, and clocks computed from the state rule. |
| 3 | Nothing beyond rung 2. | Same jurisdictions work; state archives stay a research request. |
| 4 | Federal agency table, federal templates, request tracking, deadlines from a fixed federal rule, records search on MuckRock and others, case workbench, per-page text search, cited notes and export. | Per-request clocks for extensions, tolling and the appeal window. |

a given version actually carries.

## Before you file

1. Which rung holds the record, and which body on that rung made it.
2. The body's records custodian and how they accept requests.
3. The statute you cite (the state act for rungs 1 to 3, 5 U.S.C. 552 for rung 4).
4. Record types, phrased the way a clerk can search: minutes, contracts, evaluations, correspondence, not "everything about".
5. A date range.
6. The format you want, usually electronic copies.
7. A fee cap, and a request for an itemized estimate before work starts.
8. A request that each withheld record be identified with its exemption cited.
9. For old records, the retention schedule series and the destruction log if a record is reported gone.
10. The appeal clock, written down the day you file.

## Glossary

- **Custodian.** The person or office legally responsible for a body's records and for answering requests about them.
- **Retention schedule.** The state-approved table saying how long each record series must be kept and when it may be destroyed. Early destruction is unlawful; destruction after the period is routine.
- **Glomar.** A federal response that refuses to confirm or deny that a record exists, when the fact of existence is itself exempt.
- **MDR.** Mandatory Declassification Review, a request under E.O. 13526 section 3.5 to declassify a specific record, decided outside FOIA.
- **CREST.** The CIA Records Search Tool, the collection of CIA records released under 25-year automatic declassification, online at the CIA reading room and mirrored on DocumentCloud.
- **FERPA.** The federal student-privacy law. Blocks identifiable student records, not program records.
- **Accessioned.** Transferred into the National Archives' legal custody. Once accessioned, a record is requested from NARA, not from the agency that made it.
